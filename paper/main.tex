\begin{abstract}
Mobile rover swarms communicate over wireless links whose quality changes
constantly, so the graph describing the swarm is dynamic. We study a pipeline
in which a constrained quadratic unconstrained binary optimization (QUBO)
model prunes the dynamic graph each cycle and a trust-aware message-passing
GNN assigns tasks on the pruned graph, with trust updated online from
observed outcomes. Rather than only proposing the formulation, we audit it.
First, we verify the QUBO encoding against an independent re-derivation and
exhaustive enumeration. Second, we show that penalty-encoded constrained
QUBOs are difficult for annealing-style samplers: on the actual Q matrix,
four serious solvers (local simulated annealing, D-Wave's \code{neal},
tabu search, and simulated quantum annealing) reach the exact optimum in
only 48 to 64\% of verifiable instances, while the exact linearized solve
and, as an engineering note, our feasibility-projected production annealer
solve 100\% of them (95\% against exact MILP optima on larger instances,
mean true gap below $10^{-3}$). Third, we show the uncomfortable
counterpoint: the same problem solved directly as a mixed-integer program by
CBC is exact and roughly 100 times faster than our annealer, so on classical
hardware the QUBO's role is as a hardware-target encoding, not as the
recommended solver. End-to-end, trust-aware assignment improves mission
success over random assignment by 13.8 percentage points and over a
trust-only baseline by 8.1 points (paired bootstrap 95\% CI $[+4.2, +11.7]$,
permutation $p=0.016$), with mission success now causally dependent on the
pruned topology. We report negative results with equal prominence: the QUBO
pruner does not beat a threshold pruner on raw success at ten-rover scale,
message passing adds a statistically insignificant margin over a no-graph
readout, and a cloud-trained readout does not improve over fixed weights.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Teams of small rovers coordinate over low-power radios (in our case ESP-NOW
between ESP32-class nodes), which makes the communication graph highly
dynamic: links appear, degrade, and vanish as rovers move, and nodes vary in
reliability as batteries drain and sensors fault. Allocating tasks over such
a graph raises two problems. First, running graph neural network (GNN)
inference on the full, messy graph wastes fog-tier compute and lets degraded
edges distort decisions. Second, naive pruning cannot respect global
constraints such as degree limits, processing budgets, and the requirement
that mission-critical nodes stay connected.

This paper studies a pipeline that prunes the dynamic graph with a
constrained QUBO model and then performs trust-aware GNN inference on the
surviving subgraph. Our intent from the start was to make the system
quantum-ready: the QUBO form is what quantum and quantum-inspired annealers
consume. What the work actually demonstrates, however, is broader and more
useful: a careful account of when such a formulation is solved well, when it
is not, and what each component of the pipeline contributes. Concretely, we
contribute:

\begin{itemize}
\item A constrained QUBO formulation for dynamic graph sparsification with
utility, stability, degree, budget, and connectivity terms, connectivity
enforced exactly by a single-commodity flow encoding inside the QUBO
(Section~\ref{sec:qubo}), with the implementation validated against an
independent re-derivation and exhaustive enumeration.
\item A solver-level audit of that encoding (Section~\ref{sec:solvers}):
bit-level annealers (including \code{dwave-neal}) solve only about half of
exactly verifiable instances due to a penalty-barrier effect we quantify,
while a feasibility-projected annealer solves all of them, and an exact MILP
baseline (CBC) solves the problem directly, faster than any annealer.
\item A trust-aware assignment layer with an online EWMA trust model, aging
rehabilitation, and explicit ablations isolating what the GNN contributes
over trust-only and no-graph baselines, with paired statistics
(Section~\ref{sec:gnn}).
\item An end-to-end evaluation in which mission success depends causally on
the pruned topology, including pruning baselines, latency, staleness under
cloud-amortized solving, fault recovery, sensitivity analysis, and negative
results (Section~\ref{sec:eval}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

Graph neural networks were popularized by Kipf and Welling~\cite{kipf2017}
and extended to inductive settings by Hamilton et al.~\cite{graphsage2017};
we use a lightweight trust-weighted message-passing variant suited to fog
devices. QUBO and Ising formulations are surveyed by Glover et
al.~\cite{glover2019} and Lucas~\cite{lucas2014}; simulated annealing dates
to Kirkpatrick et al.~\cite{kirkpatrick1983} and is available in production
form through D-Wave's Ocean SDK~\cite{ocean}. Our exact classical baseline
uses the CBC branch-and-cut solver from COIN-OR~\cite{cbc}, and our MILP
encoding exploits the integrality of single-commodity flow relaxations, a
standard result in network optimization~\cite{flowbook}. Multi-robot task
allocation is surveyed by Korsah et al.~\cite{mrtasurvey}; trust models for
multi-agent systems are surveyed in~\cite{trustsurvey}. When trust estimation
is isolated from the graph, the assignment problem reduces to a bandit
problem~\cite{auer2002}; we include the corresponding trust-only baseline
explicitly, since it is the strongest simple competitor. Graph
sparsification has a large classical literature~\cite{sparsification}; our
setting differs in requiring online, constraint-carrying decisions on a
dynamic graph. The mist/fog/cloud split follows Bonomi et
al.~\cite{bonomi2012}. To our knowledge, the combination of constrained QUBO
pruning with online trust-aware GNN inference for rover task allocation, and
in particular the solver-level comparison of penalty-encoded QUBO,
feasibility-projected annealing, and direct MILP for this problem, has not
been reported before.

\section{System Model}
\label{sec:model}

The deployment follows a three-tier architecture (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}).
Rovers (mist tier, ESP32-class) sense their state and exchange telemetry over
ESP-NOW. A gateway forwards telemetry to a Raspberry Pi (fog tier), which
maintains the dynamic graph, prunes it, and runs assignment. A laptop or
server (cloud tier) retrains the readout and can amortize expensive solves.

At cycle $t$ the swarm is an undirected graph $G_t=(V,E_t)$. Each edge
$e=(i,j)$ carries normalized features
$(T_e,L_e,B_e,P_e,A_e,C_e,c_e)\in[0,1]^6\times\mathbb{N}$:
trust $T_e=\min(T_i^{t-1},T_j^{t-1})$ from the previous cycle's trust model,
link quality $L_e$, battery $B_e=\min(B_i,B_j)$, proximity $P_e$,
freshness $A_e=\exp(-\Delta t_e/\tau)$ from the last successful observation
of the link, task criticality $C_e$ from the tasks running at the endpoints,
and a processing cost that models transmission and queueing time,
$c_e=\max(1,\lfloor 10\,\bar{c}_e\rceil)$ with
$\bar{c}_e = 0.1 + 0.5(1-P_e) + 0.15(\ell_i+\ell_j)$.
Node features are trust $T_i$, battery $B_i$, load $\ell_i$, and
capability $q_i$.

Mission success is causal through the pruned graph. An assigned rover
\emph{executes} a task successfully with probability equal to its hidden
reliability (gated by battery); execution outcomes drive trust updates. The
task result must then be \emph{delivered} to the root rover over the kept
subgraph, succeeding with probability equal to the weakest link quality on
the node's widest path to the root. Mission success is the conjunction. This
matters: in an earlier version of our simulator, success depended only on
node reliability, and the entire pruning layer was causally disconnected from
the metric it was supposed to improve. We consider fixing that coupling a
prerequisite for any end-to-end claim in this paper.

Each cycle executes the pipeline of Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}: build the graph,
prune edges subject to constraints, score nodes, assign tasks greedily under
per-node capacity and resource gates, observe outcomes, and update trust. A
fixed mission-critical node set (chosen once per scenario, not redefined per
cycle) anchors the connectivity constraint.

\section{Constrained QUBO Formulation}
\label{sec:qubo}

Let $O=E_t\setminus H$ be the optional edges and $H$ the hard-critical edges
fixed to 1. Let $R$ be the required node set with root $r$, $m=|R|-1$, degree
caps $D_i$, and global cost budget $K$. With binary decision variables $x_e$
for optional edges, binary-encoded integer slacks $s_i,s_C$, and integer flow
variables $f_{uv}$, the energy is

\begin{align}
H(z) =& \sum_{e\in O}(-U_e+\lambda)\,x_e
+ \eta\sum_{e\in O}(x_e-x_e^{t-1})^2 \notag\\
&+ \rho_d\sum_{i\in V}\Big(\sum_{e\in\delta_O(i)} x_e + s_i - (D_i-\deg_H(i))\Big)^2 \notag\\
&+ \rho_C\Big(\sum_{e\in O} c_e x_e + s_C - (K-c_H)\Big)^2 \notag\\
&+ \rho_F\sum_{v\in V}\big(\mathrm{in}_v-\mathrm{out}_v-b_v\big)^2
+ \rho_{FC}\sum_{e\in E}\big(f_{uv}+f_{vu}+q_e-m\,x_e\big)^2,
\label{eq:qubo}
\end{align}

where $U_e$ is the weighted edge utility with weights summing to one,
$\lambda$ is a sparsity penalty, $\eta$ a topology-stability penalty,
$b_r=-m$, $b_v=1$ for $v\in R\setminus\{r\}$ and $0$ otherwise, and
$x_e:=1$ for $e\in H$. The flow terms enforce exactly that the required set
remains connected in the selected subgraph.

The penalties satisfy $\rho_d=\rho_C=100$ and $\rho_F=\rho_{FC}=500$ against
a soft objective of magnitude at most about $1.2$ per edge. Each unit of
constraint violation costs at least $100$ but unlocks at most one edge worth
at most $1.2$, and flow violations are quadratically penalized against a
linear gain, so no violation can be profitable at any problem size. We
confirmed this exhaustively: over 38 randomly generated instances, the exact
bit-level optimum of Eq.~\eqref{eq:qubo} never contains a violated
constraint.

We want to be precise about the strength of our verification claim, because
``proven correct'' is easy to overstate. The encoding is derived from the
specification above; the implementation is then validated empirically against
an \emph{independent} re-derivation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:qubo}: on 40 instances
with 8 random states each, the matrix energy $z^{\top}Qz+\mathrm{const}$
agrees with the independent evaluation to within $2.3\times10^{-10}$. This is
strong empirical validation of the implementation, not a formal proof, and we
report it as such.

\section{Solver Analysis: The Penalty-Barrier Problem}
\label{sec:solvers}

A QUBO like Eq.~\eqref{eq:qubo} is meant to be sampled by an annealer. We
benchmarked the actual Q matrix of our instances against four serious
annealing-family solvers: local simulated annealing, D-Wave's \code{neal},
tabu search, and simulated quantum annealing (OpenJij), with the optimum
defined by an exact linearized-QUBO solve (CBC) and cross-checked against
exhaustive enumeration. From any feasible state, every single-bit flip
either breaks a constraint (penalty at least $100$) or changes the soft
objective by at most about $1.2$, so at temperatures useful for the soft
landscape, constraint-breaking moves are effectively never accepted; random
restarts of bit-level annealing returned infeasible states in 19 of 25 test
instances. Fig.~\ref{fig:solvers} shows that the difficulty is not specific
to one implementation or move set: all four samplers miss at least a third
of the instances. Our claim is deliberately narrow: \emph{on this
penalty-encoded landscape, annealing-family QUBO samplers are unreliable},
not that all QUBO methods must fail; the exact solver handles the same
matrices without difficulty.

For the production pipeline we therefore do not rely on Q-matrix sampling.
Our production solver, \emph{projected edge annealing}, moves in the space
of feasible edge subsets instead of the space of bit strings: a move toggles
one optional edge or swaps two edges, and the candidate is projected back to
feasibility by an exact repair procedure that recomputes slacks and a
spanning-tree flow; unprojectable moves are rejected. The objective is
still exactly $H(z)$, restricted to the feasible manifold where penalty
terms vanish; the QUBO remains the specification, and the Q matrix is still
exported for hardware backends. A deterministic polish alternates
single-toggle and pairwise-swap passes to a local fixpoint, and random
restarts are seeded with degree-cap-aware random spanning trees, because
greedy edge-by-edge growth can never cross the infeasible prefix states
that connectivity constraints create. We deliberately exclude this solver
from the scientific comparison above, since its move set is richer than the
samplers'; we report only that it reaches 100\% exact on the same instances
(about $2.4\times10^4$ energy evaluations and 2.1\,s per solve), as an
engineering note rather than a claim about QUBO solving.

\subsection{The strong classical baseline: direct MILP}

The fairest question to ask of a QUBO pipeline is why not solve the original
constrained problem directly. The soft objective of Eq.~\eqref{eq:qubo} is
linear in $x$, the stability term is linear for binary variables, and the
single-commodity flow relaxation is integral, so the problem is a
mixed-integer linear program. We solve it exactly with CBC~\cite{cbc}. On 20
instances with up to 40 optional edges, the projected annealer reaches the
MILP optimum in 95\% of cases (mean normalized gap $7\times10^{-5}$, worst
$1.4\times10^{-3}$), using the normalized gap
$(H_{\mathrm{SA}}-H^{*})/\max(1,|H^{*}|)$. On 25 small instances it remains
100\% exact against exhaustive enumeration, and on 20 larger instances (up to
817 binary variables) its gap against the best of four annealer seeds is
6.9\% on average.

The counterpoint is just as important: CBC solves these instances in about
15\,ms, against about 2.1\,s for the annealer. On classical hardware, direct
MILP is both exact and roughly 100 times faster. What remains for the QUBO
encoding is precisely its original purpose: it is the input format of quantum
and quantum-inspired annealing hardware, and the projected annealer is the
strong classical stand-in until such hardware is evaluated. We state this
plainly rather than overselling the formulation.

\section{Trust-Aware GNN Inference}
\label{sec:gnn}

On the pruned graph, each node is scored for each task by a two-layer
trust-weighted message-passing network. With $h_i^{(0)}=[T_i,B_i,1-\ell_i,q_i]$,
\[
m_i = \frac{\sum_j a_{ij} h_j}{\sum_j a_{ij}},\qquad
h_i' = \mathrm{ReLU}\big(W_s h_i + W_n m_i + b\big),\qquad
s_i = \sigma\big(w^{\top} h_i^{(2)} + \beta\big),
\]
where $a_{ij}=T_{ij}L_{ij}$ weights messages by link trust and quality. The
self-weight (0.8) dominates the neighborhood weight (0.2); nodes below
per-task trust, battery, or compute thresholds are gated to zero, with a
deadlock fallback that relaxes the trust gate when it would leave a task
unassigned. Scores are modulated by $(0.5+0.5\,p_i)$, where $p_i$ is the
weakest-link quality on node $i$'s path to the root, so the assigner prefers
strong delivery paths without letting the root absorb every task.

Trust itself is dynamic. After each cycle, an assigned node's trust follows
an EWMA, $T_i \leftarrow \alpha T_i + (1-\alpha)\,\mathrm{obs}_i$, while
unobserved nodes age toward the prior,
$T_i \leftarrow T_i + \delta(0.5-T_i)$. Aging rehabilitates a rover after a
single unlucky failure; without it we observed permanent blacklisting, and
without per-task load feedback we observed overload lockout. Hidden
reliability in the simulator drifts over time and is spatially correlated
(nearby rovers share conditions), so the trust model must track a moving,
structured target rather than estimate a stationary Bernoulli mean. The
readout $(w,\beta)$ can be trained offline by logistic regression on
collected outcomes; we collect training data with a high exploration rate so
that a random-policy, selection-bias-free subset exists (Section~\ref{sec:training}).

\section{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

All experiments run on the open harness in the repository
(\code{battle\_test.py}, \code{simulation.py}, \code{scale\_runner.py};
486 checks passing) on an AMD Ryzen 7 5800H (16 threads, 8\,GB RAM, Python
3.14, NumPy with the reference CBLAS). The simulator is a synthetic dynamic
graph world inspired by rover networking; it is not a wireless channel model,
and we report it as such.

\subsection{Pruning baselines and the honest pruner table}

Table~\ref{tab:pruners} compares the QUBO pruner against three baselines over
identical scenarios (10 rovers, 20 cycles, 10 seeds, drifting reliability).
Two results deserve emphasis. First, mission success now depends on the
pruned topology by construction (delivery over the kept graph), so the table
measures a real causal effect. Second, the QUBO pruner \emph{loses} on raw
success: $-4.3$ points against the threshold pruner, paired bootstrap 95\% CI
$[-7.0, -1.5]$, permutation $p=0.028$. The QUBO's edge-local utility optimum
removes redundancy that bottleneck-path delivery needs; keeping 84 to 100\%
of edges wins on delivery at this scale. What the QUBO buys is not success
but guarantees: degree caps, a hard budget, and exact connectivity of the
mission set, which the baselines cannot promise. Its trust estimates are also
the best of the table (Spearman 0.260, AUC 0.662). Whether the guarantee is
worth 4 success points is a deployment decision, and we report it as one.

\subsection{Assignment ablations with paired statistics}
\label{sec:ablation}

To isolate what the GNN contributes, we compare four assignment policies on
identical scenarios: random among eligible rovers, trust-only (highest
trust, the bandit-style baseline), a no-graph logistic readout on raw node
features, and the full trust-aware GNN (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). The GNN
beats trust-only by 8.1 points with a paired bootstrap CI excluding zero and
a significant permutation test; it beats random by 13.8 points. The margin of
the full GNN over the no-graph readout is $+1.7$ points but not significant
($p=0.74$): at ten-rover density, most of the value is in the trust model and
the path-aware gating, and message passing adds a small, not yet significant,
extra. We consider this the honest answer to ``is the GNN necessary'': the
graph helps, modestly, and the claim at this scale is limited to that.

\subsection{Staleness and cloud amortization}

A 1.2\,s solve cannot run synchronously on a fog device in a fast-changing
network, so the architecture amortizes: solve every $k$ cycles (cloud),
reuse the stale decision (intersected with the live graph) between solves.
Table~\ref{tab:staleness} shows that reusing a two-cycle-old decision costs
under one success point while halving solve load; at $k=4$ and above the
measurement is dominated by seed variance, which we say rather than drawing a
curve through noise. The guarantee bookkeeping matters here: the simulator
counts how often each relaxation level is used (full constraints, no budget,
budget only, unconstrained, stale reuse), so the coverage of the connectivity
guarantee is measurable rather than asserted.

\subsection{Trust quality, training, and faults}
\label{sec:training}

Learned trust tracks hidden reliability at Spearman 0.21 to 0.26 and
classifies above-median rovers at AUC 0.59 to 0.66 during operation; the
QUBO-pruned arm achieves the best values. Training the readout on the
unbiased exploration subset (78 samples) does not improve held-out success
over fixed weights (36.7\% vs 32.8\%, difference $-3.9$ points, CI
$[-11.7,+11.7]$, $p=0.49$): with a selection-bias-free but small dataset, the
trained readout is not better, and we report the null result rather than
tune until it looks good. Under the scripted fault scenario (most reliable
rover degraded plus a packet-loss spike), success returns to its pre-fault
baseline within 3 to 4 cycles after the fault expires, with edge lifecycle
tracking (active, suspect, probation, removed) keeping pruned links
observable.

\subsection{Sensitivity}

The projected annealer's exact-solve rate is 87\% on a fixed instance set and
stays at 87\% under every perturbation we tested: trust weight scaled
$1.5\times$, uniform utility weights, penalties divided by 5 or multiplied by
5, sparsity penalty raised to 0.3, and stability penalty raised to 0.6. The
solver is not fragile to the encoding's free parameters, which matters
because those parameters are currently chosen by hand.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

Three findings cut against the naive version of our own pitch, and we
consider them the most useful part of the study. First, penalty-encoded
constrained QUBOs are easy to write and hard to sample: three annealing
variants solve barely half of verifiable instances, so papers reporting such
encodings without a solver-level audit risk reporting formulation errors as
model behavior. Second, on classical hardware the whole QUBO detour is
strictly dominated by direct MILP, which is exact and 100 times faster; the
encoding's justification is hardware targeting, not classical performance.
Third, at small fleet sizes the pruner costs far more than the inference it
protects and does not beat a threshold pruner on raw success; the
architecture is justified by guarantees and by scale, not by small-scale
latency or accuracy.

Limitations. Statistical power is limited (8 to 10 seeds per arm); the
message-passing margin is not significant; the simulator is synthetic, with
no wireless channel model, no hardware-in-the-loop, and no quantum hardware
evaluation (the Q matrix is exported but untested on a QPU); the dense Q
matrix bounds the practical fleet size at tens of rovers; the scaling study
enforces connectivity only up to 20 rovers and does not pretend otherwise;
the trained-readout experiment is a null result on limited data; and the
utility weights and penalties are hand-chosen, with sensitivity analysis but
no principled tuning procedure.

\section{Conclusion}

We built and audited a pipeline that couples constrained QUBO graph pruning
with trust-aware GNN inference for dynamic rover networks. The encoding is
derived cleanly and validated exhaustively on finite instances. Once the
solver respects feasibility, the formulation is solved exactly on every
instance small enough to enumerate, and to within $10^{-3}$ of exact MILP
optima on larger ones, while the direct MILP remains the better classical
solver. End-to-end, trust-aware assignment significantly improves mission
success over random and trust-only baselines in a simulator where success
depends causally on the pruned topology. Equally important, the audit
produced three negative results with real content: annealing samplers
struggle on penalty-encoded constraints, MILP dominates the QUBO on classical
hardware, and the pruner trades a few success points for hard guarantees at
small scale. The codebase, harness, and all reported numbers are open and
reproducible.
