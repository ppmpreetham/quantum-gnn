\begin{abstract}
Rovers that coordinate over low-power radio see a communication graph that
changes from one cycle to the next. We study a pipeline that allocates tasks
over such a graph in two steps. A constrained quadratic unconstrained binary
optimization (QUBO) model first prunes the graph to a working subgraph that
respects per-rover link caps, a processing budget and exact connectivity of
the mission rovers. A lightweight trust-aware graph neural network (GNN) then
scores the rovers on that subgraph, and trust is learned online from task
outcomes. Our main interest is in checking how well each part works. The
encoding agrees with an independent re-derivation to within
$2.3\times10^{-10}$. On the actual Q matrices, four annealing-family samplers
(local simulated annealing, D-Wave's \code{neal}, tabu search and simulated
quantum annealing) find the exact optimum on only 48 to 64\% of verifiable
instances, because the penalties that make violations unprofitable also block
the samplers' moves. A feasibility-projected annealer is exact on all of them
and matches exact mixed-integer optima on 95\% of larger instances, but the
mixed-integer program solved directly by CBC is also exact and about 100 times
faster. On classical hardware the QUBO is therefore best seen as an encoding
for annealing hardware. In a closed-loop simulator where mission success
depends causally on the kept links, trust-aware assignment beats a trust-only
baseline by 8.1 percentage points (paired bootstrap 95\% CI $[+4.2,+11.7]$,
$p=0.016$) and random assignment by 13.8 points. The QUBO pruner is the only
arm that never violates a constraint, but it gives up 2 to 4 points of raw
success against constraint-aware repaired baselines, and no level of a
ten-level constraint-pressure sweep reverses this. Logging which constraints
were enforced also showed that our own earlier comparison used a budget that
could never be met. Finally, the unchanged pipeline runs in a Gazebo and
ROS~2 world and drives an interactive 3D simulation engine.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Small, inexpensive rovers are attractive for survey, inspection and
monitoring work in places where there is no fixed communication
infrastructure. A team of such rovers has to organize itself. Every few
seconds some node must decide which rover takes which task, and that decision
can only be as good as the information that reaches it. In our setting the
information travels over ESP-NOW links between ESP32-class boards. Each rover
hears only a few teammates, and every link changes as rovers move, batteries
drain and terrain gets in the way. The communication network is therefore a
graph whose edges appear, weaken and disappear from cycle to cycle.

Two problems follow. Running GNN inference on the full graph wastes the small
compute budget of the fog device and lets poor links distort the scores. At
the same time, simple pruning rules that judge one edge at a time cannot
respect constraints that depend on the whole kept graph, such as degree
limits, a processing budget, or the requirement that mission-critical rovers
stay connected. We address both by pruning with a constrained QUBO, the input
format of quantum and quantum-inspired annealers, and then assigning tasks
with a trust-aware GNN on the kept graph (Fig.~\ref{fig:pruning}).

We started with the aim of building a quantum-ready pipeline. Along the way
the more useful outcome turned out to be a careful account of what each
component contributes when it is measured against the strongest alternatives
we could find, including ones that beat our pipeline. The contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
\item A constrained QUBO for dynamic graph pruning with utility, stability,
degree, budget and connectivity terms, in which connectivity is enforced
exactly by a single-commodity flow encoding. The implementation is checked
against an independent re-derivation and exhaustive enumeration, and a
five-rover example shows the connectivity trap it avoids
(Section~\ref{sec:qubo}).
\item A solver audit on the actual Q matrices. Four annealing-family samplers
miss a third or more of verifiable optima because of a penalty barrier that
we quantify. A feasibility-projected annealer removes the barrier, and an
exact MILP baseline solves the problem directly and faster than any annealer
(Section~\ref{sec:solvers}).
\item A trust-aware assignment layer with an online trust model, aging,
resource gates and delivery awareness, evaluated against trust-only, no-graph
and random baselines with paired statistics (Sections~\ref{sec:gnn}
and~\ref{sec:ablation}).
\item A causal evaluation that includes constraint-aware repaired baselines,
delivery-critical precision and recall, a constraint-pressure sweep, cloud
amortization, scaling and fault recovery, together with an audit note on a
budget that was silently relaxed in our earlier results
(Section~\ref{sec:eval}).
\item Two deployments of the unchanged pipeline: a containerized Gazebo
Harmonic and ROS~2 Jazzy world, and a browser-based 3D simulation engine with
collision-free differential-drive motion and live paired comparisons
(Section~\ref{sec:deploy}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Graph neural networks and graph pruning.}
Kipf and Welling~\cite{kipf2017} made graph convolutional networks popular
and Hamilton et al.~\cite{hamilton2017} extended them to inductive settings.
Several works prune the input graph or the weights of a GNN. STEP~\cite{li2023step}
learns a self-supervised pruner for large dynamic graphs from graph
statistics alone. CGP~\cite{liu2024cgp} sparsifies structure, features and
weights together during training, Wang et al.~\cite{wang2022} remove edges
according to their effect on the loss, and Peng et al.~\cite{peng2022} study
sparse training of GNN weights. These methods aim at model or training
efficiency on static benchmarks and do not enforce hard constraints. Spectral
sparsification~\cite{spielman2011} preserves the spectrum of a graph but also
has no notion of degree caps, budgets or a required set.

\paragraph{QUBO models and their solvers.}
Glover et al.~\cite{glover2019} and Lucas~\cite{lucas2014} survey QUBO and
Ising formulations. Simulated annealing~\cite{kirkpatrick1983} is available in
production form through D-Wave's Ocean tools~\cite{ocean}, quantum
annealing~\cite{kadowaki1998} is emulated classically by simulated quantum
annealing in OpenJij~\cite{openjij}, and tabu search~\cite{glover1989} is a
strong local-search alternative. Our exact baselines use the CBC
branch-and-cut solver~\cite{cbc} and the integrality of single-commodity flow
relaxations~\cite{ahuja1993}.

\paragraph{Task allocation, trust and fog computing.}
In the taxonomy of Korsah et al.~\cite{korsah2013} our problem has
single-task robots, single-robot tasks and instantaneous assignment that is
recomputed every cycle. Ahanger et al.~\cite{ahanger2023} use a
quantum-inspired optimizer to allocate IoT tasks across edge and fog nodes,
and MAG-Q~\cite{verma2026} combines graph attention, actor-critic learning and
a QUBO forwarding module for offloading in clustered fog networks. Trust
appears in load balancing for edge computing~\cite{ali2023} and in UAV task
offloading~\cite{guo2021}, and computational trust models are reviewed by
Sabater and Sierra~\cite{sabater2005}. When trust is estimated without the
graph, choosing a rover becomes a bandit problem~\cite{auer2002}, so we
include a trust-only baseline. The mist, fog and cloud split follows Bonomi
et al.~\cite{bonomi2012}.

As far as we know, constrained QUBO pruning has not been combined with
online trust-aware GNN inference for rover task allocation before, and we are
not aware of a solver-level comparison of penalty-encoded sampling,
feasibility-projected annealing and direct MILP for this kind of problem.

\section{System Model}
\label{sec:model}

The deployment has three tiers (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). Rovers in the mist tier
sense their state and exchange telemetry over ESP-NOW. A gateway forwards the
telemetry to a Raspberry Pi in the fog tier, which maintains the graph,
prunes it and assigns tasks within the cycle deadline. A laptop or server in
the cloud tier retrains the readout and can take over expensive solves.

At cycle $t$ the fleet is an undirected graph $G_t=(V,E_t)$ whose edges are
the pairs of rovers that can currently exchange packets. Each edge
$e=(i,j)$ carries normalized features: trust $T_e=\min(T_i^{t-1},T_j^{t-1})$
from the previous cycle, link quality $L_e$, battery $B_e=\min(B_i,B_j)$,
proximity $P_e$, freshness $A_e=\exp(-\Delta t_e/3)$ since the link was last
heard, and the criticality $C_e$ of the tasks at its ends. A processing cost
models transmission and queueing time,
$c_e=\max(1,\lfloor10\,\bar c_e\rceil)$ with
$\bar c_e=0.1+0.5(1-P_e)+0.15(\ell_i+\ell_j)$. The usefulness of an edge is
the weighted utility
\begin{equation}
U_e=0.25\,T_e+0.25\,L_e+0.10\,B_e+0.10\,P_e+0.15\,A_e+0.15\,C_e,
\label{eq:utility}
\end{equation}
which every pruning baseline also uses. Each rover has trust $T_i$, battery
$B_i$, load $\ell_i$ and capability $q_i$.

\paragraph{Causal mission model.}
Pruning can only be judged if the kept links affect the outcome, so a task
succeeds only when two events both happen. The assigned rover $i$
\emph{executes} the task with probability equal to its hidden reliability,
provided its battery is above 15\%. The result must then be \emph{delivered}
to the root rover $r$ over the kept subgraph, which succeeds with probability
\begin{equation}
p_i=\max_{\pi:\,i\to r}\ \min_{e\in\pi}L_e,
\label{eq:delivery}
\end{equation}
the weakest link on the widest path from $i$ to $r$ ($p_r=1$, and $p_i=0$ when
no path exists). Trust is updated from execution outcomes, so it estimates
reliability, while delivery ties success to the pruning decision. An earlier
version of our simulator had no delivery step, so success did not depend on
the kept graph at all. Every end-to-end result in this paper uses the coupled
model.

Each cycle runs the pipeline of Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}: build the graph,
prune it under the constraints, score the rovers, assign tasks greedily under
per-rover capacity, observe outcomes and update trust. The mission-critical
set is chosen once per scenario and anchors the connectivity constraint.

\section{Constrained QUBO Formulation}
\label{sec:qubo}

Let $H$ be the hard-critical edges, which are always kept, and
$O=E_t\setminus H$ the optional edges. Let $R$ be the required set with root
$r$ and $m=|R|-1$, let $D_i$ be the degree caps and $K$ the budget. With a
binary $x_e$ for each optional edge, binary-encoded integer slacks $s_i$,
$s_C$ and $q_e$, and integer flows $f_{uv}$ on directed arcs, the energy is
\begin{align}
H(z) =& \sum_{e\in O}(-U_e+\lambda)\,x_e
+ \eta\sum_{e\in O}(x_e-x_e^{t-1})^2 \notag\\
&+ \rho_d\sum_{i\in V}\Big(\sum_{e\in\delta_O(i)} x_e + s_i - (D_i-\deg_H(i))\Big)^2
+ \rho_C\Big(\sum_{e\in O} c_e x_e + s_C - (K-c_H)\Big)^2 \notag\\
&+ \rho_F\sum_{v\in V}\big(\mathrm{in}_v-\mathrm{out}_v-b_v\big)^2
+ \rho_{FC}\sum_{e\in E}\big(f_{uv}+f_{vu}+q_e-m\,x_e\big)^2,
\label{eq:qubo}
\end{align}
with $b_r=-m$, $b_v=1$ for $v\in R\setminus\{r\}$, $b_v=0$ otherwise, and
$x_e=1$ for $e\in H$. The sparsity penalty is $\lambda=0.08$ and the stability
penalty $\eta=0.15$. Because $x_e^2=x_e$, the stability term is linear in
$x_e$.

\begin{proposition}
The two flow terms are zero for some choice of flows and slacks if and only
if every required rover is connected to $r$ in the subgraph of kept edges.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
If every required rover is connected to $r$, take a spanning tree of the
root's component and send one unit from $r$ to each required rover along its
tree path. No arc carries more than $m$ units, conservation holds with the
given supplies, and the slack $q_e\in[0,m]$ closes each capacity equality.
Conversely, zero penalties mean that $m$ units leave $r$, one unit is absorbed
at every other required rover, and flow only uses kept edges, so each
required rover is reachable from $r$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Penalty dominance.}
We set $\rho_d=\rho_C=100$ and $\rho_F=\rho_{FC}=500$. The soft objective of
one edge is at most about $1.2$ in size, while one unit of violation costs at
least $100$ and frees at most one edge. Flow violations are penalized
quadratically against a linear gain. No violation can therefore pay off, at
any problem size, and on 38 random instances the exact bit-level optimum of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:qubo} never contained a violated constraint.

\paragraph{A worked example.}
Consider five rovers, the root $0$ and rovers $1$ to $4$, with seven links in
range. Link $0$--$1$ is hard-critical because it is rover~1's only tether,
the required set is $\{0,2,3,4\}$, the degree caps are $(3,2,3,2,2)$ and the
budget is $K=12$. Even this small instance needs 61 binary variables (six edge
bits, 42 flow bits, 9 degree-slack bits and 4 budget-slack bits), although the
decision has only $2^6=64$ outcomes. Enumerating all of them gives just three
feasible subsets. The optimum keeps $\{0\text{--}1,0\text{--}2,2\text{--}3,
2\text{--}4\}$ at energy $-1.750$, and the other two feasible subsets score
$-1.555$. Thirty of the 61 infeasible subsets break only connectivity, and the
best of them, $\{0\text{--}1,0\text{--}2,1\text{--}2,2\text{--}3\}$, has a
\emph{lower} soft energy of $-1.965$ because it keeps the attractive link
$1$--$2$, but it cuts rover~4 off from the root. A pruner that looks only at
soft scores, or checks connectivity afterwards, would choose it. In the QUBO
the flow terms push it above $+498$. The cheapest subset that breaks a degree
or budget constraint sits at $+97.475$, a barrier of $99.2$ above the optimum,
which is almost a hundred times wider than the band of feasible energies
(Fig.~\ref{fig:ladder}). The matrix energy of the optimum, the projected
annealer of Section~\ref{sec:solvers} and the exact MILP all give the same
answer.

\paragraph{Verification.}
A second implementation evaluates every term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:qubo} directly
from the specification, without building $Q$. On 40 instances with 8 random
states each, the matrix energy $z^{\top}Qz+\mathrm{const}$ agrees with it to
within $2.3\times10^{-10}$. This is empirical evidence about the
implementation and not a formal proof. It does let us attribute a poor answer
in the next section to the solver and not to the encoding.

\section{Solving the QUBO}
\label{sec:solvers}

\subsection{The penalty barrier}

A QUBO like Eq.~\eqref{eq:qubo} is written to be sampled by an annealer. From
a feasible state, a single bit flip either keeps feasibility, which changes the
soft objective by at most about $1.2$, or breaks a constraint and raises the
energy by at least $100$. Keeping feasibility is rare, because slack and flow
bits have to change together with an edge bit. At a temperature that is
useful for the soft landscape, a constraint-breaking move is accepted with
probability around $e^{-100/\tau}$, which is effectively zero, so the
sampler cannot move between feasible states that differ by more than one
coordinated change. In our tests, random restarts of bit-level annealing
ended in infeasible states on 19 of 25 instances.

To check that this is not a weakness of one implementation, we gave the
actual Q matrices of 25 small instances (four to seven rovers, at most sixteen
optional edges, tight caps) to four samplers: our bit-level simulated
annealer, D-Wave's \code{neal}, tabu search and simulated quantum annealing
from OpenJij. The ground truth is the exact optimum of the linearized Q matrix
solved by CBC, cross-checked against enumeration on instances with at most 60
variables. Each sampler's answer is projected to the edge set it selects
before scoring, which helps the samplers. All four miss at least a third of
the instances (Fig.~\ref{fig:solvers}). Our claim is limited to this
penalty-encoded landscape. The exact solver handles the same matrices without
any trouble.

\subsection{Projected edge annealing}

The production solver works on edge sets instead of bit strings. A move
either toggles one optional edge or swaps a kept edge for an unkept one, with
equal probability. Each candidate is projected back to feasibility: the degree
and budget slacks are computed in closed form, a spanning-tree flow is rebuilt
from the root to every required rover, and a move that cannot be completed is
rejected. In a feasible state all penalties vanish, so the solver still
minimizes exactly $H(z)$, only restricted to feasible states, and the Q matrix
can still be exported to hardware. Restarts begin from random spanning trees
that respect the degree caps, because growing a tree greedily one edge at a
time can get stuck when every partial tree is infeasible. The first restart may
start from the previous decision restricted to the live graph. After the
temperature schedule a deterministic polish alternates single toggles and
swaps until no move improves the energy. Since every candidate is feasible,
the search never meets the penalty walls.

We keep this solver out of the sampler comparison because its moves are
richer than bit flips. As an engineering result, it is exact on all 25
verifiable instances (about $2.4\times10^4$ energy evaluations and 2.1~s per
solve), its exact-solve rate on a fixed instance set stays at 87\% under every
sensitivity perturbation we tried (Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}), and on 20
larger instances with up to 817 binary variables its mean gap to the best of
four annealer seeds is 6.9\%.

\subsection{The relaxation ladder}
\label{sec:ladder}

In a live system the constraints can be jointly infeasible, for example when
the budget is below the cheapest connected subgraph. The production pruner
therefore tries a sequence of problems and records which one it used in every
cycle (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}). The required set is the mission roster
restricted to rovers reachable from the root, and unreachable mission rovers
are counted instead of being dropped silently. One defect is worth reporting:
the warm start counts as one restart, so with a single restart and an
infeasible warm start no random tree is ever tried, and a solvable instance

\subsection{The exact MILP baseline}

The obvious question for any QUBO pipeline is why the constrained problem is
not solved directly. The soft objective is linear, the stability term is
linear for binary variables, and the single-commodity flow relaxation is
integral for integral data~\cite{ahuja1993}, so the problem is a
mixed-integer linear program. We solve it with CBC and accept an answer only
when optimality is proven. On 20 instances with up to 40 optional edges, the
projected annealer reaches the MILP optimum in 95\% of cases, with mean
normalized gap $7\times10^{-5}$ and worst gap $1.4\times10^{-3}$, where the gap
is $(H_{\mathrm{SA}}-H^{*})/\max(1,|H^{*}|)$. CBC, however, takes about 11 to
15~ms per instance against about 2.1~s for the annealer. On classical
hardware the MILP is exact and roughly 100 times faster. The QUBO is still
the input format of quantum and quantum-inspired annealers, and the projected
annealer is a good classical stand-in for that encoding until such hardware is
tested, but it is not the solver we would recommend on a classical machine.

\section{Trust-Aware GNN Inference and Assignment}
\label{sec:gnn}

On the kept graph each rover is scored by two layers of trust-weighted
message passing. Starting from $h_i^{(0)}=[T_i,B_i,1-\ell_i,q_i]$,
\[
m_i=\frac{\sum_j a_{ij}h_j}{\sum_j a_{ij}},\qquad
h_i'=\mathrm{ReLU}\big(W_sh_i+W_nm_i+b\big),\qquad
s_i=\sigma\big(w^{\top}h_i^{(2)}+\beta\big),
\]
with $a_{ij}=T_{ij}L_{ij}$, $W_s=0.8\,I$, $W_n=0.2\,I$, $b=0$ and readout
weights $w=(0.60,0.15,0.10,0.15)$. Pruned edges carry no messages, which is how
the pruning decision enters the scores. With equal self and neighbour weights,
neighbour information washed out the differences between rovers in a cluster,
so the self weight dominates.

The raw score is then adjusted three times. A rover is gated to zero if its
trust is below the task minimum (0.25), its battery below 15\% or its free
compute below the task need (0.3), and if the trust gate would leave a task
with no candidate the gate is relaxed for that task. Mission-critical tasks
multiply the score by $(0.5+0.5\,T_i)$. Finally every score is multiplied by
$(0.5+0.5\,p_i)$ with $p_i$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:delivery}, which prefers
rovers with strong delivery paths without letting the root take every task.
Tasks are then assigned greedily from the highest score down, with one task
per rover per cycle, and each assignment raises the rover's load, which decays
over later cycles. With probability $\varepsilon=0.15$ a task goes to a
random eligible rover instead, and these exploration samples are tagged.

Trust follows an exponentially weighted moving average,
$T_i\leftarrow\alpha T_i+(1-\alpha)\,\mathrm{obs}_i$ with $\alpha=0.5$, for
rovers that were assigned, while the others drift back towards the prior,
$T_i\leftarrow T_i+\delta(0.5-T_i)$ with $\delta=0.1$. Without aging, one
unlucky failure would leave a rover at 0.25 on the trust gate for good, and
without the load feedback the most trusted rover received every task until it
ran out of compute; we saw both before adding the fixes. Hidden reliability
in the simulator is spatially correlated and drifts over time, so trust has to
track a moving target. Pruned links are still tracked: a small state machine
marks links as active, suspect, in probation or removed, and links in
probation get a freshness floor of 0.4 so they can be re-evaluated when
conditions improve. The readout $(w,\beta)$ can be trained in the cloud by
logistic regression on the exploration-tagged samples, which are free of
selection bias (Section~\ref{sec:trust}). The whole stage costs about 0.2~ms
at ten rovers and about 1~ms at fifty.

\section{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\paragraph{Setup.}
All experiments use the open harness in the repository, built around
\code{simulation.py} and covered by a suite of roughly 490 automated checks
in \code{battle\_test.py}. The
simulator is a synthetic dynamic-graph world with random-walk mobility,
distance-based link quality with noise, 5\% packet loss that makes link
knowledge stale, hidden reliability and scripted faults. It is not a wireless
channel model. Unless stated otherwise, scenarios have 10 rovers and 20
cycles. Differences between arms are paired per seed, confidence intervals
are 95\% paired bootstrap intervals with 10\,000 resamples~\cite{efron1993},
and $p$-values come from sign-flip permutation tests with 20\,000
permutations. Timings were measured on an AMD Ryzen 7 5800H (16 threads,
8~GB RAM) with Python 3.14 and NumPy on its reference linear algebra library.

\paragraph{Audit note: the original budget never bound.}
Our earlier pruner tables used a fixed budget of $K=15$ units. The relaxation
log shows that at this budget the production pruner used the \emph{no-budget}
rung in 319 of 320 cycles, because 15 units is less than the cost of any
connected subgraph at ten rovers. The QUBO row of those tables was therefore
in effect unconstrained. This is the kind of silent failure the log was meant
to catch, and we now treat logging the rung as a requirement rather than
asserting feasibility. All results below use the binding budget of
Table~\ref{tab:pruners}, where the QUBO solves with every constraint in 320
of 320 cycles.

\paragraph{What the guarantees cost.}
At the binding budget the QUBO pruner is behind threshold+repair by 4.1
points (CI $[-6.4,-1.9]$, $p=0.004$) and behind top-$K$+repair by 4.2 points
(CI $[-7.0,-1.5]$, $p=0.017$). Against the constraint-blind threshold pruner
the gap is 5.7 points (CI $[-8.7,-2.9]$, $p=0.002$), so comparing only with
constraint-blind pruners would overstate the cost by about 1.6 points. The
gap that remains is still significant. In return, the QUBO is the only arm
with no violations at all; the repaired baselines still leave 1.1 to 3.1
degree violations and 0.8 to 0.9 connectivity misses per run. Its learned
trust has the best Spearman correlation (0.295 against 0.251 to 0.271), but
not the best AUC. The delivery metrics show where it loses. Its
delivery-failure rate is 4.4 points higher than threshold+repair (CI
$[+1.4,+8.0]$) and 11.8 points higher than top-$K$ (CI $[+7.6,+16.0]$), and
its recall of delivery-critical links is 0.67, against 0.82 for the repaired
baselines and 0.93 for the threshold pruner. Only the constraint-blind
top-$K$, which keeps half of the links, has lower recall (0.56). The
edge-local utility ranks a link by its own quality and not by how much
delivery depends on it, so the optimum sometimes drops the redundancy that
bottleneck paths need.

\subsection{Trust quality, training and faults}
\label{sec:trust}

Learned trust follows hidden reliability with a Spearman correlation of 0.24
to 0.30 and an AUC of 0.62 to 0.68 during operation. Comparing these numbers
across pruners is not clean, because each pruner produces its own stream of
observed outcomes, and a common unbiased stream would be needed for a fair
ranking. Training the readout on the 78 exploration-tagged samples did not
improve held-out success over the fixed weights (32.8\% against 36.7\%, a
difference of $-3.9$ points, CI $[-11.7,+11.7]$, $p=0.49$). We report this as
a null result; more exploration data would be needed to train the readout
properly. In the scripted fault scenario, where the most reliable rover is
degraded for four cycles and a packet-loss spike follows, trust in that rover
falls, assignment moves away from it, and success returns to its earlier
level 3 to 4 cycles after the fault ends.

\subsection{Sensitivity}
\label{sec:sensitivity}

On a fixed instance set the projected annealer is exact on 87\% of instances,
and it stays at 87\% under every perturbation we tested: the trust weight
scaled by 1.5, uniform utility weights, penalties divided or multiplied by 5,
the sparsity penalty raised to 0.3 and the stability penalty raised to 0.6.
This matters because these parameters are currently set by hand.

\section{Deployments}
\label{sec:deploy}

\paragraph{Gazebo and ROS~2.}
The planner also runs as a ROS~2 node against a physics simulation. Four
differential-drive rovers ($0.9\times0.65\times0.25$~m, 3~kg) are spawned in a
Gazebo Harmonic world~\cite{koenig2004}, their odometry is bridged into ROS~2
Jazzy~\cite{macenski2022}, and the
whole stack runs in one Docker container started with a single command. Once
per second the node builds the graph from the live positions (5~m radio range,
all four rovers required, root 0, degree cap 3, budget 25 units), prunes it,
assigns a critical and a routine task, and publishes its status and
assignments as JSON. Link quality depends only on distance, because Gazebo
models geometry and not radio propagation, and a short annealing schedule
solves the four-rover problem in 12 to 16~ms. On the spawn line-up the pruner
keeps all five candidate links in every cycle, which is optimal here because
every link is healthy and neither the caps nor the budget bind. Trust visibly
steers assignment: rover~3 holds the critical task while it is trusted, fails
in cycle~3, drops from 0.92 to 0.50, and the task moves to rover~1 from the
next cycle. The node reads odometry but does not yet publish velocity
commands, so the rovers stay at their spawn poses and the graph does not
change during a run.

\paragraph{An interactive 3D simulation engine.}
To let people watch the pipeline make decisions, we built a browser-based 3D
engine on three.js~\cite{threejs}. The browser handles time, motion and
rendering, and a Python planner service wraps the unchanged closed-loop
simulator: once per planning cycle the browser sends the live rover positions
and the current parameters, the positions replace the simulator's random walk,
and the service returns kept links, trust, assignments and outcomes. Rovers
move with differential-drive kinematics at a fixed step of $1/120$~s. They are
updated one at a time, and a translation is accepted only if, for every other
rover at its current position, the new distance is at least $2R_b$ or does not
shrink; otherwise the rover only turns in place. This rule keeps every pair
of footprint circles at least $\min(2R_b,d)$ apart after each step, so rovers
never overlap. Every object class is drawn with one instanced call, and the
whole scene costs seven draw calls whatever the fleet size. For live
comparisons the service runs paired followers for trust-only, random and
no-graph assignment from the same seed and initial state, and for solver
comparisons it hands the exact Q matrix of the current cycle, with the same
warm start, to bit-level annealing, \code{neal}, tabu search or simulated
quantum annealing. The engine lists only baselines that our pipeline beats in
its comparison menu, and it names the MILP and the threshold pruner, which
beat it, in a note next to the menu. The motion code has seventeen property
tests and the planner service nineteen.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

Three findings run against the simple version of our own pitch, and we think
they are the most useful results of the study. First, penalty-encoded
constrained QUBOs are easy to write and hard to sample: four annealing-family
samplers miss a third or more of verifiable optima, so a paper that reports
such an encoding without checking solver output against exact answers may
mistake a solver failure for a property of the model. Second, on classical
hardware the QUBO route is dominated by a direct MILP that is exact and about
100 times faster, so the encoding is justified by hardware targeting and not
by classical performance. Third, at small fleet sizes the pruner costs far
more time than the inference it protects and gives up 2 to 4 points of
success against constrained baselines. Its value is the guarantee, which
matters when a violation is not acceptable.

The study has clear limits. Statistical power is modest, with 6 to 16 seeds
per arm depending on the experiment, and the margin of message passing over
the no-graph readout is not significant. Trust-quality comparisons across
pruners are confounded by selection. The simulator is synthetic, with no
wireless channel model, no hardware in the loop and no run on quantum
hardware; the Q matrices are exported but untested on a quantum processor. The
dense Q matrix limits practical fleets to tens of rovers, and the scaling
study enforces connectivity only up to 20 rovers. The trained readout is a
null result on limited data, and the utility weights and penalties are set by
hand, with a sensitivity analysis but no tuning procedure. In the Gazebo world
the rovers do not yet move. A four-rover ESP32 testbed has been designed but
not built.

The natural next steps follow from these limits: running the exported Q
matrices on a quantum annealer and comparing against the certified optima,
adding a delivery-aware term to the utility so that links that bottleneck
paths depend on are valued, replacing the dense flow encoding with a sparse or
hierarchical one, moving the Gazebo rovers with the engine's controller, and
building the physical testbed.

\section{Conclusion}

We built a pipeline that prunes a dynamic rover graph with a constrained QUBO
and assigns tasks with a trust-aware GNN, and we audited each part against
exact answers and strong baselines. The encoding is verified on finite
instances, and once the solver respects feasibility it is exact on every
instance small enough to enumerate and within $1.4\times10^{-3}$ of exact MILP
optima on larger ones, while the direct MILP remains the better classical
solver. Trust-aware assignment improves mission success significantly over
trust-only and random baselines in a simulator where success depends on the
kept links. The audit also produced negative results that we consider part of
the contribution: annealing samplers struggle with penalty-encoded
constraints, the MILP dominates the QUBO on classical hardware, and the pruner
trades 2 to 4 points of success for its guarantees at every pressure level we
tested. It even found a problem in our own earlier comparison, whose budget
was silently relaxed in 319 of 320 cycles. The code, test harness, Gazebo
world, 3D engine and all reported numbers are available in the repository.
