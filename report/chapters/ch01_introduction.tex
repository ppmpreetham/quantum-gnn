\section{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

\subsection{Motivation}
\label{sec:intro-motivation}

Teams of small, inexpensive rovers are an increasingly realistic answer to a
class of problems where a single large machine is the wrong tool. In planetary
exploration, a cluster of metre-scale scouts can survey a lava tube or a
crater rim in parallel, covering in hours what one flagship rover covers in
weeks, and the loss of one unit degrades the mission instead of ending it.
Closer to home, disaster response presents the same structure: after an
earthquake or a chemical release, a team of disposable ground robots can map
voids, sample air, and relay survivor signals through rubble that no human
and no single large vehicle can enter. Both settings share three properties
that drive everything in this report. The environment is unknown and
changing, the robots are cheap and therefore individually unreliable, and
there is no infrastructure: the team must carry its own network with it.

That network is the central object of our study. We consider rovers that
communicate over short-range, low-power radios of the ESP-NOW class: link
budgets of a few hundred milliwatts, useful ranges of tens of metres, and
packet delivery probabilities that fluctuate with distance, occlusion, and
interference. As the rovers move, the set of pairs that can hear each other
changes continuously, so the communication topology is not a fixed design
choice but a \emph{dynamic graph} that the mission software inherits anew
every control cycle. Two consequences follow. First, the full graph is rarely
the graph you want to compute on: it contains weak, stale, and expensive
links that add noise to every downstream decision while consuming radio and
processor budget. Second, the rovers themselves differ in dependability, and
that dependability drifts. A unit with a degrading motor controller or a
corrupt sensor may still look perfectly reachable on the radio graph while
quietly failing every task it is given.

These observations motivate the two mechanisms at the heart of the project.
\emph{Link pruning} treats the working communication graph as a decision
variable: rather than accepting whatever topology the radios happen to
provide, the system selects, once per cycle, a smaller subgraph that is
provably connected where it matters, respects per-rover degree and
processing budgets, and favours links that are trusted, fresh, and cheap.
\emph{Trust} treats reliability as a learned quantity: each rover carries a
trust score, updated online from observed task outcomes, which biases both
which links survive pruning and which rover is offered which task. A rover
that keeps failing is not argued with; it is simply routed around, until its
later successes earn its way back. The two mechanisms are coupled, and the
coupling is the point: pruning decides who talks to whom, trust decides who
is believed, and both feed a task allocator whose successes and failures
become the evidence for the next round of trust updates.

\subsection{Problem statement}
\label{sec:intro-problem}

Each control cycle, the system faces two coupled discrete decisions. The
first is \emph{which links to keep}. Given the current dynamic graph, a
vector of per-link features (trust of the endpoints, measured link quality,
battery levels, proximity, freshness of the last successful exchange,
criticality of pending traffic, and the processing cost of maintaining the
link), and a set of hard requirements (mission-critical nodes must remain
reachable from a designated root, no rover may exceed its degree limit, and
the total processing cost may not exceed a global budget), choose a subset
of edges that maximises a weighted utility while satisfying every hard
requirement. The second decision is \emph{which rover does which task}.
Given the pruned graph, score every rover for every pending task and
produce an assignment that respects per-rover capacity. The decisions are
coupled because the scoring runs \emph{on the pruned graph}: a rover cut off
by pruning cannot be reached by the assignment, and an assignment that
concentrates load changes the trust and battery features that the next
pruning round will see. A deliberate design rule follows from this coupling:
connectivity is never sacrificed for utility, since a beautifully scored
task allocation is worthless if the winning rover cannot be sent its orders.

The problem is a constrained combinatorial optimisation with quadratic
structure (the utility of keeping two links is not always the sum of their
individual utilities, because redundant paths and shared endpoints
interact), and it must be solved repeatedly, online, under a time budget,
on hardware far weaker than a workstation.

\subsection{Why a QUBO, and why we say so honestly}
\label{sec:intro-why-qubo}

We encode the link-selection decision as a \emph{quadratic unconstrained
binary optimisation} problem, or QUBO: an energy function over binary
variables in which every hard constraint becomes a penalty term large enough
that violating it is never worth the utility it could buy. The attraction of
this encoding is portability. A QUBO is the native input format of quantum
annealers and of a growing family of dedicated classical samplers, so a
single formulation can be submitted unchanged to a D-Wave-class device, to
the D-Wave \code{neal} simulated annealer, to a tabu search, or to an
Ising-machine simulator such as OpenJij. If annealing hardware matures, the
encoding is already waiting for it.

Honesty, however, is a design requirement of this project, not a garnish. On
classical hardware, an exact mixed-integer linear program solved with CBC
finds provably optimal solutions to the same constrained problem roughly two
orders of magnitude faster than our annealer, and the off-the-shelf QUBO
samplers we benchmarked find the exact optimum on our actual penalty matrix
in only about half to two thirds of instances. We report these numbers in
full, in the body of the report, with the same prominence as our positive
results. The QUBO formulation is justified by its hardware trajectory and
its clean separation of modelling from solving, not by a false claim of
classical superiority. Where a classical exact method wins, we say it wins.

\subsection{The mist, fog, and cloud deployment vision}
\label{sec:intro-tiers}

The architecture is organised around three computational tiers. The
\emph{mist} tier is the rovers themselves: microcontrollers with just enough
compute to run radio exchange, local sensing, and task execution. The
\emph{fog} tier is a single Raspberry Pi-class board riding with the team.
It runs the two latency-critical inference stages each cycle: the production
annealer that solves the pruning QUBO, and a deliberately tiny trust-aware
message-passing graph neural network (GNN), implemented in pure NumPy, that
scores rovers on the pruned graph. Nothing on the fog tier requires a
library heavier than NumPy, because nothing on that tier may assume a
network connection. The \emph{cloud} tier, reachable opportunistically,
does the heavy and patient work: training the GNN readout weights on
collected mission logs, and amortising expensive solves so that a fresh
QUBO need not be built from scratch every cycle. This division is not
decorative. It dictates engineering constraints that the reader will see
resurface throughout the report: the GNN must run without a deep-learning
framework, the annealer must be restartable and seedable for
reproducibility, and every cloud dependency must have a graceful fog-tier
fallback.

\subsection{An audit-first philosophy}
\label{sec:intro-audit}

This project is built as an audit of itself. Every quantitative claim in
this report is backed by an executable check, and the check survives in the
project's hostile test harness long after the claim is written. The QUBO
encoding is verified against exhaustive enumeration of all feasible edge
subsets on small instances and against an exact MILP on larger ones, so the
penalty matrix is never taken on faith. Four off-the-shelf QUBO samplers are
benchmarked directly on the penalty matrix our formulation actually
produces, rather than on a convenient synthetic instance. Statistical claims
about mission success come with paired bootstrap confidence intervals and
permutation p-values, and comparisons that fail to reach significance are
labelled as such in the tables where they appear. Most importantly, negative
results are reported with equal prominence. Our learned GNN readout does not
beat its hand-tuned weights; the QUBO pruner loses on raw mission success to
a trivially simple threshold pruner at one fleet scale; classical MILP
dominates the annealer. Each of these findings is presented, explained, and
kept, because a report about trustworthy robots that hides its own failures
would be making the opposite of its point.

\subsection{Contributions}
\label{sec:intro-contributions}

The project makes the following contributions.

\begin{itemize}
  \item A constrained QUBO formulation of communication-graph pruning for
    dynamic rover networks, unifying multi-feature link utility with hard
    connectivity, degree, and processing-budget constraints in a single
    annealer-compatible energy function.
  \item A feasibility-projected simulated annealer that moves only in the
    space of valid edge sets, repairing every candidate against the hard
    constraints, and that matches exact enumeration on small instances and
    achieves 95\% exactness against MILP ground truth on larger ones.
  \item A solver-level audit benchmarking four off-the-shelf QUBO samplers
    (bit-level simulated annealing, D-Wave \code{neal}, tabu search, and an
    OpenJij-based Ising sampler) on the actual penalty matrix, quantifying
    how far generic samplers fall short of feasibility-aware search.
  \item A trust-aware GNN scoring stage, small enough for pure NumPy
    inference on Raspberry Pi-class fog hardware, integrated with online
    per-rover trust updates driven by observed task outcomes.
  \item A causal end-to-end simulation harness in which hidden ground-truth
    reliability drives outcomes, so that mission success genuinely depends
    on the pruned topology rather than merely correlating with it, with
    paired bootstrap and permutation statistics throughout.
  \item A physics integration using Gazebo Harmonic and ROS 2 Jazzy, plus a
    browser-based fleet console built on a custom three.js engine with a
    Python backend, allowing live inspection and in-place tuning of the
    full pipeline.
  \item A set of deliberately reported negative results, including the null
    outcome of readout training and the raw-success deficit of QUBO pruning
    against a simple threshold baseline, each analysed rather than
    suppressed.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The per-cycle pipeline}
\label{sec:intro-pipeline}

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarises the loop that every chapter of this
report elaborates. Each control cycle begins from the current dynamic
communication graph. The QUBO builder encodes the pruning decision as a
penalty matrix, and the production annealer solves it in feasibility-projected
edge space, yielding the working graph. The trust-aware GNN scores rovers on
that working graph, a capacity-aware greedy stage assigns the pending tasks,
and outcomes are observed. The trust model absorbs the outcomes as evidence,
updates every rover's score, and the updated features feed the next cycle's
graph construction, closing the loop.

\subsection{Report roadmap}
\label{sec:intro-roadmap}

The report is organised into fourteen chapters, summarised in
Table~\ref{tab:roadmap} and sketched here. Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction} is
the present chapter. Chapter 2 reviews related work on multi-robot task
allocation, trust models for robotic teams, and QUBO and annealing methods,
positioning our combination of the three. Chapter 3 fixes notation and
formalises the system model: the dynamic graph, the link and node features,
and the two coupled per-cycle decisions. Chapter 4 derives the constrained
QUBO formulation term by term, including the penalty-domination argument
that makes hard constraints safe. Chapter 5 presents the production annealer,
its feasibility projection, spanning-tree restarts, and polish phase, and
analyses its evaluation budget. Chapter 6 reports the solver audit: exact
enumeration and MILP ground truth, the four off-the-shelf samplers, and the
sensitivity sweeps. Chapter 7 describes the trust model and the trust-aware
GNN, including the null result on learned readout weights. Chapter 8
specifies the causal simulation harness, the hidden reliability field, and
the statistical machinery. Chapter 9 gives the mission-level results:
ablations, pruner comparisons, staleness margins, and the negative results.
Chapter 10 covers the mist, fog, and cloud deployment architecture and its
engineering constraints. Chapter 11 presents the Gazebo Harmonic and ROS 2
Jazzy physics integration. Chapter 12 describes the browser-based fleet
console, its three.js rendering engine, and its live tuning interface.
Chapter 13 collects the threats to validity and the limitations we consider
binding. Chapter 14 concludes and maps the most promising directions for
future work.
