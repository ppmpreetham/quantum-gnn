\section{Background and Foundations}\label{ch:background}

This chapter collects the mathematical and algorithmic machinery on which the
rest of the report rests. Our aim is not to survey any single field
exhaustively, but to build a shared vocabulary: what a dynamic rover network
is, why its task-allocation problem is combinatorial, how the QUBO form
encodes that problem, which solvers we use to attack it, and how we judge the
answers with exact methods and statistics. Each later chapter cites back to
the definitions given here.

\subsection{Dynamic Graphs and Rover Networks}

We model the fleet at any instant as an undirected graph
$G_t = (V, E_t)$, where each vertex $v \in V$ is a rover and an edge
$\{u, v\} \in E_t$ exists whenever rovers $u$ and $v$ are within radio range
of one another. Because the rovers move, the edge set $E_t$ changes from
cycle to cycle: rovers that could exchange messages one minute may be out of
range the next, and links appear or vanish as the fleet spreads out, regroups,
or loses a rover to a fault. A graph whose edge set is a function of time in
this sense is a \emph{dynamic graph}.

Dynamism has three practical consequences for our project. First, decisions
are perishable: a routing or allocation plan computed on $G_t$ may be invalid
on $G_{t+1}$ even if no rover has failed, simply because the topology moved
on. Second, every decision must be recomputed on a deadline; a solver that is
exact but too slow is useless if the graph has changed by the time it
finishes. Third, information is local and stale: a rover knows its own state
precisely but learns about distant rovers only through relayed messages, so
any score or estimate carried through the network has a freshness that
decays with time. The pipeline in this project is built around these three
facts. Each cycle takes the current graph $G_t$, prunes it to a sparser
subgraph that still supports the mission, scores the rovers on the pruned
graph, assigns tasks, and folds the observed outcomes back into a trust model
that shapes the next cycle's decisions.

Two features of the graph matter throughout. The first is
\emph{connectivity}: a pruned subgraph is useful only if every rover that
must receive a task remains reachable from a designated root rover, which we
formalize below as a flow requirement. The second is \emph{degree}: a rover
with a low-power radio cannot maintain arbitrarily many simultaneous links,
so each vertex carries a degree limit. Both features are hard constraints,
not preferences, and they drive the constrained optimization formulation of
the next section.

\subsection{Constrained Combinatorial Optimization and the QUBO Form}

Many planning problems ask us to choose a subset of objects (edges, routes,
assignments) that maximizes some utility while respecting limits (budget,
capacity, connectivity). Because the choices are discrete, these are
\emph{combinatorial optimization} problems, and most interesting instances
are NP-hard: no algorithm is known that solves every instance in time
polynomial in the input size.

A quadratic unconstrained binary optimization problem, or \emph{QUBO}, is the
following canonical form. Let $z \in \{0,1\}^n$ be a vector of binary
decision variables, $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ a symmetric matrix, and
$c \in \mathbb{R}$ a constant offset. The objective is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qubo}
  E(z) \;=\; z^{\top} Q z + c
  \;=\; \sum_{i} Q_{ii}\, z_i \;+\; \sum_{i<j} Q_{ij}\, z_i z_j \;+\; c,
\end{equation}
and we seek $z^{\star} = \arg\min_z E(z)$. The diagonal entries $Q_{ii}$
price each variable on its own; the off-diagonal entries $Q_{ij}$ price
pairs, rewarding or penalizing the co-selection of $i$ and $j$. Since $z_i^2
= z_i$ for binary variables, linear terms fold into the diagonal. The QUBO
form is attractive for two reasons: it is the native input format of quantum
annealers and of most generic binary samplers, and it forces the modeler to
make every trade-off explicit as a number in one matrix.

\subsubsection{Penalty encoding of hard constraints}

A QUBO is \emph{unconstrained}, so the hard constraints of the underlying
problem must be folded into the objective. The standard device is a
\emph{penalty term}: for each constraint, we add to $Q$ a quadratic form
that is zero exactly when the constraint holds and is strongly positive
otherwise. For example, a degree limit $\sum_{j \sim i} z_{ij} \le d_i$ at
vertex $i$ becomes a penalty $P \bigl(\max\{0, \sum_{j \sim i} z_{ij} -
d_i\}\bigr)^2$ after introducing integer slack variables, themselves encoded
as a short binary expansion. In this project we encode four constraint
families this way: per-rover degree limits, a global edge budget, single-commodity
flow conservation for connectivity, and flow capacity on each edge.

The penalty weight $P$ must be chosen so that \emph{breaking a constraint is
never worth it}. Formally, let $\Delta_{\text{soft}}$ be the largest possible
swing of the soft (constraint-free) part of the objective between any two
candidate solutions, and let $g_{\min} > 0$ be the smallest nonzero violation
of any constraint. Then any $P > \Delta_{\text{soft}} / g_{\min}$ guarantees
that every minimizer of $E$ is feasible. If $P$ is too small, the sampler
will happily buy soft utility at the price of an infeasible graph; if $P$ is
needlessly huge, the penalty terms flatten the energy landscape into
plateaus separated by cliffs, which slows heuristic search. In this project
the soft terms are normalized so that one selected edge is worth at most
about one unit of utility, while a single constraint violation costs at
least $100$ units. The gap of two orders of magnitude places every feasible
solution strictly below every infeasible one, and our invariant tests verify
this dominance property directly on the assembled matrix $Q$.

\subsection{Simulated and Quantum Annealing}

\subsubsection{Simulated annealing}

Simulated annealing (SA) is a local-search heuristic inspired by the slow
cooling of a solid. Starting from an initial candidate $z$, it repeats the
following move: propose a neighbor $z'$ (for instance by flipping one bit),
compute the energy change $\Delta E = E(z') - E(z)$, and accept the move
with the \emph{Metropolis criterion}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:metropolis}
  \Pr(\text{accept}) \;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    1, & \Delta E \le 0,\\[2pt]
    \exp(-\Delta E / T), & \Delta E > 0,
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $T$ is a control parameter called the \emph{temperature}. Downhill
moves are always taken; uphill moves are taken with a probability that
shrinks both with the size of the climb and as $T$ falls. High temperature
early in the run lets the search wander across valleys and escape shallow
local minima; low temperature late in the run freezes it into a local
minimum, ideally a deep one. The schedule $T_k = T_0 \, r^{k}$ with cooling
rate $r$ slightly below one is the workhorse choice and the one used
throughout this project; Figure~\ref{fig:annealing-schedule} shows the
shape. Because a single run is a gamble on its starting point, practical
implementations use \emph{restarts}: several independent runs from different
initial states, keeping the best result. All annealers in this report also
expose a random seed, which lets us reproduce every experiment exactly.

Plain bit-flip SA on a penalty-laden QUBO has a structural weakness: most
bit strings are infeasible, and penalty cliffs separate feasible regions, so
a random walk spends its budget among infeasible states. This observation
motivates the \emph{projected} annealer developed later in this report,
whose moves live entirely in the space of feasible edge sets. Chapter~4
describes it; here we note only that the Metropolis rule is unchanged and
the projection enters in how neighbors are proposed.

\subsubsection{Quantum annealing and simulated quantum annealing}

Quantum annealing replaces thermal hopping over energy barriers with quantum
tunneling through them. Physically, a system of coupled qubits is prepared
in the ground state of a simple \emph{driver} Hamiltonian and then slowly
morphed into a \emph{problem} Hamiltonian whose ground state encodes the
QUBO optimum; if the evolution is slow enough and hardware noise permits,
the system ends in or near the optimum. We do not have quantum hardware in
this project. Instead we use \emph{simulated quantum annealing} (SQA), a
classical path-integral Monte Carlo method that mimics the quantum system:
the bit string is replicated into several coupled \emph{Trotter slices},
and a transverse field parameter plays the role of temperature, being
annealed downward over the run. SQA runs on ordinary hardware and, like
thermal SA, offers no optimality certificate, so it enters our study as one
more sampler to audit, alongside a local bit-flip SA we implement ourselves,
a production-grade SA library, and a tabu search library.

\subsection{Mixed-Integer Linear Programming and Exact Ground Truth}

Heuristic samplers tell us the best energy they found; they never tell us
how far that energy is from the true optimum. To measure that gap we need an
exact solver, and ours comes from \emph{mixed-integer linear programming}
(MILP). A MILP minimizes a linear objective subject to linear constraints,
with some variables required to be integer. The workhorse algorithm is
branch and bound with linear-programming relaxations, and we use the open
source solver CBC through a Python modeling layer.

Two exact models serve as ground truth in this project. The first is a MILP
of the original edge-selection problem itself, with binary edge variables,
integer slacks, and continuous flow variables. The second is an exact solve
of the assembled QUBO matrix via the standard linearization: each quadratic
term $z_i z_j$ with $Q_{ij} \neq 0$ is replaced by an auxiliary binary
variable $y_{ij}$ plus the constraints $y_{ij} \le z_i$,
$y_{ij} \le z_j$, and $y_{ij} \ge z_i + z_j - 1$, which force
$y_{ij} = z_i z_j$ at any binary assignment. Both models are small enough at
our test scales for CBC to prove optimality outright, and we count a solve as
valid only when CBC certifies it; otherwise the instance is skipped rather
than trusted.

One subtlety makes the first model exact despite its continuous flows. A
single-commodity flow formulation that ships one unit of demand from a root
to each required vertex has, for integer demands, an \emph{integral}
polytope: every extreme point of its feasible region assigns integer flow
values. Intuitively, the flow polytope is the intersection of conservation
equations whose constraint matrix is totally unimodular, so relaxing
integrality of the flows changes nothing about the optimum. We may therefore
declare flow variables continuous, which keeps the model small and lets CBC
solve it in milliseconds, while losing no exactness. We verified this
empirically as well: the MILP optimum coincides with brute-force enumeration
of all feasible edge subsets on every small instance we tested.

\subsection{Message-Passing Graph Neural Networks}

A graph neural network (GNN) computes vector representations of vertices by
repeatedly mixing each vertex's features with those of its neighbors. One
\emph{message-passing round} for vertex $i$ with feature vector
$x_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ on a graph with weighted adjacency
$A = (a_{ij})$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:message-passing}
  h_i \;=\; \sigma\!\Bigl( W_{\text{self}}\, x_i \;+\;
  \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} a_{ij}\, W_{\text{nbr}}\, x_j \Bigr),
\end{equation}
where $W_{\text{self}}$ and $W_{\text{nbr}}$ are learned or hand-set weight
matrices, $\mathcal{N}(i)$ is the neighbor set of $i$, and $\sigma$ is the
elementwise sigmoid $\sigma(t) = 1/(1 + e^{-t})$, which squashes its input
into $(0,1)$. The hidden state $h_i$ now carries information about the
one-hop neighborhood. Stacking more rounds propagates information farther,
at the price of smoothing and compute; this project uses a single round,
which is cheap enough for a low-power onboard computer and suffices because
the pruning stage has already removed most long-range redundancy from the
graph. A final \emph{readout} maps hidden states to per-vertex scores that
rank rovers for task assignment. Figure~\ref{fig:message-passing} sketches
one round.

In our setting the input features are trust, battery level, available
compute, and capability, and the adjacency weights come from the pruned
topology. Two properties of this design matter later. First, the model is
purely feed-forward arithmetic on small matrices, so it runs in microseconds
per rover and imposes essentially no load on the fog tier. Second, because
scoring happens \emph{after} pruning, the GNN's view of the world is exactly
the communication graph the mission will actually use, which ties allocation
quality causally to pruning quality.

\subsection{Trust Models for Multi-Agent Systems}

A rover that has failed silently, or whose radio is degrading, should receive
fewer critical tasks, even if it looks attractive on paper. Trust models make
this notion quantitative. Following the trust literature, notably the survey
of Sabater and Sierra (2005), we treat trust as a reputation score built
from direct interaction outcomes, updated incrementally, and allowed to age.

Our model is an exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA). Each rover $i$
carries a scalar trust $\tau_i \in [0,1]$. When rover $i$ is observed
succeeding ($o = 1$) or failing ($o = 0$) on a task, its trust updates as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ewma}
  \tau_i \leftarrow (1 - \alpha)\,\tau_i + \alpha\, o,
\end{equation}
with learning rate $\alpha \in (0,1)$. Recent evidence dominates: a single
bad outcome lowers trust immediately, while recovery requires a run of
successes, which is the asymmetry we want for mission-critical assignment.
Complementing the update is \emph{aging}: trust that has not been refreshed
by a recent observation relaxes toward a neutral prior, so stale evidence
counts for less and a long-unobserved rover is neither trusted nor
distrusted outright. Trust feeds the pipeline in two places, as a vertex
feature for the GNN and as an edge feature for the pruning stage, so
reputation influences both which links survive and which rovers are chosen.

\subsection{Statistical Tools Used Throughout the Report}

Because every experiment involves random seeds, hidden reliability draws, and
stochastic solvers, single-run numbers mean little. We rely on four tools.

\paragraph{Paired bootstrap confidence intervals.} To compare two methods
run on the same seeds, we form the per-seed difference in the metric of
interest, resample the differences with replacement $10{,}000$ times, and
take the $2.5$ and $97.5$ percentiles of the resampled means as a $95\%$
confidence interval. Pairing on seeds removes instance-difficulty variance
and sharpens the comparison.

\paragraph{Permutation tests.} For a $p$-value we use an exact-style
permutation test on the paired differences: under the null hypothesis that
the two methods are exchangeable, the sign of each difference is arbitrary,
so we flip signs at random across $20{,}000$ permutations and measure how
often the permuted mean is at least as extreme as the observed one. This
makes no normality assumption.

\paragraph{Spearman rank correlation.} When we ask whether a predicted score
orders rovers the way realized outcomes do, we use Spearman's rank
correlation, which compares orderings rather than values and is therefore
robust to the arbitrary scale of the scores.

\paragraph{Normalized optimality gap.} To grade a sampler against exact
ground truth $H^{\star}$, we report
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normgap}
  \text{gap} \;=\; \frac{H_{\text{SA}} - H^{\star}}{\max\bigl(1,\; |H^{\star}|\bigr)},
\end{equation}
which is nonnegative since $H^{\star}$ is minimal, equals zero exactly when
the sampler is optimal, and is protected from division blow-ups when the
optimal energy is near zero.
