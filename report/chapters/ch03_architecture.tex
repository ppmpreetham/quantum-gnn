\chapter{System Model and Architecture}
\label{ch:arch}

\section{Design Goals}

We fixed four goals for the architecture before choosing any algorithm. The
first is that decisions have to respect the structure of the whole graph,
since link caps, the budget and connectivity can only be judged on the full
set of kept edges. The second is that the fog tier has to stay cheap, so
everything that runs on the Raspberry Pi in every cycle should finish within a
few milliseconds. The third is that learning has to feed back into the next
cycle: trust learned from outcomes is used in the following cycle, and nothing
inside one cycle depends on its own output. The last goal is that every claim
should be checkable. Each stage can be tested against an independent
reference, and mission success in the simulator depends causally on the
decisions that are being evaluated.

\section{Three-Tier Deployment}

The deployment follows the three-tier split of Fig.~\ref{fig:arch:tiers}.
Rovers in the mist tier sense their state and exchange telemetry over
ESP-NOW. A gateway forwards the telemetry over Wi-Fi to the fog tier, a
Raspberry Pi that maintains the dynamic graph, prunes it, scores the rovers
and assigns tasks. The cloud tier, a laptop or server reached over MQTT,
retrains the GNN readout and runs the heavy optimisation experiments. Task
assignments travel back down the same path.

The tier assignment is a design decision: rovers have too little compute for
anything beyond sensing and radio, the fog tier must meet a per-cycle deadline,
and the cloud has none. Section~\ref{sec:res:staleness} quantifies a middle
ground in which the cloud solves every few cycles and the fog device reuses the
latest decision in between.

\section{Graph Model and Features}

At cycle $t$ the fleet is the undirected graph $G_t=(V,E_t)$, with an edge
whenever two rovers are within radio range. Table~\ref{tab:arch:features}
lists the features attached to every edge and every rover. All continuous
features are normalised to $[0,1]$. Trust and battery on an edge take the
minimum over the two endpoints, so a link is only as good as its weaker end,
which is the conservative choice for communication reliability.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Edge and rover features used by the pruner and the GNN.}
\label{tab:arch:features}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l l X@{}}
\toprule
Symbol & Name & Definition \\
\midrule
$T_e$ & edge trust & $\min(T_i^{t-1},T_j^{t-1})$, from the previous cycle's trust model \\
$L_e$ & link quality & measured quality of the last observation, with noise \\
$B_e$ & edge battery & $\min(B_i,B_j)$ \\
$P_e$ & proximity & $1-d_{ij}/d_{\max}$ for distance $d_{ij}$ and radio range $d_{\max}$ \\
$A_e$ & freshness & $\exp(-\Delta t_e/\tau)$ since the last successful observation \\
$C_e$ & task criticality & largest criticality of the tasks at the two endpoints \\
$c_e$ & processing cost & $\max\bigl(1,\lfloor 10\,\bar c_e\rceil\bigr)$ integer budget units \\
\midrule
$T_i$ & rover trust & online EWMA estimate (Chapter~\ref{ch:gnn}) \\
$B_i$ & battery & state of charge \\
$\ell_i$ & load & fraction of compute in use; $1-\ell_i$ is available compute \\
$q_i$ & capability & static hardware capability score \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The raw processing cost gives the budget a physical meaning. Transmission time
grows with distance and endpoint queues add processing time, so
\begin{equation}
\bar c_e=0.1+0.5\,(1-P_e)+0.15\,(\ell_i+\ell_j),
\label{eq:arch:cost}
\end{equation}
and the integer cost $c_e$ lies between 1 and 10. Because every edge costs at
least one unit, any spanning tree on $n$ rovers costs at least $n-1$ units; the
3D engine of Chapter~\ref{ch:engine} uses this bound to warn when a chosen
budget can never be met.

The usefulness of an edge is summarised by a weighted utility
\begin{equation}
U_e=0.25\,T_e+0.25\,L_e+0.10\,B_e+0.10\,P_e+0.15\,A_e+0.15\,C_e,
\label{eq:arch:utility}
\end{equation}
whose weights sum to one, so $U_e\in[0,1]$. The same utility is used by every
pruning baseline, which keeps comparisons between pruners fair.

\section{One Pipeline Cycle}

Each control cycle executes the pipeline of Fig.~\ref{fig:arch:cycle}. The
graph builder turns telemetry into $G_t$ with fresh features. Next, the pruner
picks a working subgraph that meets the constraints, and the GNN uses that
pruned graph to score each rover for each task that is waiting. The assignment
stage then matches tasks to rovers greedily, without giving any rover more
than it can handle. At the end of the cycle we see which tasks succeeded and
update the trust values.

\section{The Mission Model}

Pruning the graph only makes sense if the choice of kept links can actually
change how a mission turns out. For this reason the mission model treats a
successful task as two linked events.

\begin{definition}[Execution and delivery]
When rover $i$ is given a task, it \emph{executes} the task with a
probability equal to its hidden reliability, as long as its battery is above
15\%. After that, the result has to be \emph{delivered} to the root rover
through the kept subgraph. Delivery succeeds with a probability given by the
delivery factor
\[
p_i=\max_{\pi:\,i\to r}\ \min_{e\in\pi} L_e,
\]
which is the quality of the weakest link on the \emph{widest} path $\pi$ from
$i$ to the root $r$ in the kept graph. By definition $p_r=1$, and $p_i=0$ when
no path exists. The task only counts as a success if both events happen.
\end{definition}

Trust is updated from execution outcomes only, which means it is an estimate
of reliability.
Delivery depends on the kept edges, so every pruning decision has a real,
measurable effect on mission success. An earlier version of the simulator
lacked this coupling: success depended on rover reliability alone and the
whole pruning layer was causally disconnected from the metric it was meant to
improve. Restoring the coupling was a precondition for every end-to-end claim
in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

\section{Software Organisation}

The software mirrors the stages of Fig.~\ref{fig:arch:cycle} and adds the
verification and simulation layers around them. Table~\ref{tab:arch:software}
lists the components by responsibility; they are written in Python with
NumPy, except the 3D engine, which is written in TypeScript.

\small

\toprule
Component & Responsibility \\
\midrule
QUBO builder & Assembles the Q matrix with slack and flow variables, checks inputs, repairs edge sets to feasibility and exports the matrix \\
Solvers & Projected edge annealing (production) and bit-level simulated annealing (baseline) \\
Exact baselines & Direct MILP of the constrained problem and an exact linearised solve of the Q matrix, both with CBC \\

\section{Physical Testbed Concept}

The intended physical testbed is four rovers on a flat surface, each an
ESP32-class board on a small differential-drive chassis, with ESP-NOW links
whose quality varies with distance (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch:testbed}). A degraded
link, such as the one between R3 and R4 in panel~(a), is exactly the kind of
edge the pruner is meant to drop; panel~(c) shows one kept graph without it,
in which all four rovers remain connected. The testbed has not yet
been built; all quantitative results in this report come from simulation, and
the Gazebo world of Chapter~\ref{ch:gazebo} reproduces the same four-rover
line-up in a physics engine.
