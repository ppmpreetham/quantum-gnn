\chapter{Solving the QUBO: the Penalty Barrier and Projected Annealing}
\label{ch:solvers}

\section{Annealing a Penalty-Encoded QUBO}

A QUBO such as Eq.~\eqref{eq:qubo:energy} is written to be sampled by an
annealer, classical or quantum. The natural first solver is therefore a
bit-level simulated annealer that flips one bit of $z$ at a time and accepts
uphill moves with probability $\exp(-\Delta E/\tau_{\mathrm{SA}})$. For a
symmetric $Q$ the energy change of flipping bit $k$ is cheap to compute,
\begin{equation}
\Delta E_k=2\,(1-2z_k)\,(Qz)_k+Q_{kk},
\label{eq:sol:delta}
\end{equation}
and maintaining the vector $Qz$ incrementally makes each proposal $O(N)$.
The experiments of this chapter show that this solver, and three serious
alternatives, fail on our problem in a specific and quantifiable way, even
though the encoding was verified to be correct.

\section{The Penalty Barrier}

Consider any feasible state. Every single-bit flip either keeps the state
feasible, changing the soft objective by at most about $1.2$, or breaks a
constraint, raising the energy by at least $100$. A flip of an edge bit that
keeps feasibility is rare, because the slack and flow bits must change
together with it; most single flips therefore break something. The
probability of accepting a constraint-breaking move at temperature
$\tau_{\mathrm{SA}}$ is
\[
\Pr[\text{accept}]=e^{-100/\tau_{\mathrm{SA}}},\qquad
e^{-100}\approx3.7\times10^{-44},\qquad e^{-10}\approx4.5\times10^{-5}.
\]
At $\tau_{\mathrm{SA}}=1$, a temperature already hot for a soft landscape of
width about one, the acceptance probability is astronomically small; even at
$\tau_{\mathrm{SA}}=10$ it is only $4.5\times10^{-5}$ per attempt, and at that
temperature the soft landscape is pure noise. A bit-level annealer therefore
cannot travel between feasible regions: it either freezes in the basin of
its starting state or wanders through infeasible space and returns an
infeasible answer. Fig.~\ref{fig:sol:barrier} sketches the situation.

\section{Benchmarking Four Serious Samplers}

To rule out a weak implementation as the explanation, the actual Q matrices
of our instances were given to four annealing-family solvers. The benchmark
uses 25 small instances with four to seven rovers and at most sixteen
optional edges, generated with tight degree and budget caps so that the
constraints bind. The ground truth $H^{*}$ of every instance is the exact
optimum of the linearised Q matrix solved by CBC (Chapter~\ref{ch:exact}),
cross-checked against exhaustive enumeration of all edge subsets on instances
with at most 60 QUBO variables. Each sampler's answer is projected to the
feasible edge set it selects before scoring, which favours the samplers. The
samplers were bit-flip SA (20 restarts, cooling 0.98, feasible warm start),
\emph{neal} (100 reads), tabu search (10 reads, 200~ms limit) and OpenJij SQA
(25 reads); Fig.~\ref{fig:sol:bars} plots their exact-solve rates.

The difficulty is not specific to one implementation or one move set: all
four samplers miss at least a third of the instances, including the
production \emph{neal} sampler, a deterministic tabu search and a
quantum-inspired sampler. Random restarts of the bit-level annealer, without
a feasible warm start, returned \emph{infeasible} states in 19 of the 25
instances: the samplers are not converging to slightly wrong optima, they
are failing to find legal states at all. The claim is deliberately narrow.
On this penalty-encoded landscape, annealing-family QUBO samplers are
unreliable; the same matrices are solved exactly, without difficulty, by an
exact method.

\section{Projected Edge Annealing}

The production solver developed in this project, \emph{projected edge
annealing}, keeps the QUBO as the specification of the problem but changes
the space in which the search moves: it moves between feasible \emph{edge
sets} instead of between bit strings.

\subsubsection{Moves} A move either toggles one optional edge or swaps two
edges (one in, one out), each with probability one half. Swaps matter
because many improvements, such as replacing one tree edge by a better one,
pass through an infeasible intermediate state when done one edge at a time.

\subsubsection{Projection} Every candidate edge set is projected back onto
the feasible manifold by an exact repair procedure: degree and budget slacks
are computed in closed form from the selected edges, and a spanning-tree flow
from the root to every required rover is recomputed. If no feasible
completion exists, for example because the candidate disconnects a required
rover, the move is rejected outright.

\subsubsection{Objective} For a feasible state every penalty term vanishes,
so the energy reduces to the soft objective, which costs $O(|O|)$ to
evaluate. The solver therefore still minimises exactly $H(z)$, restricted to
the feasible manifold, and the matrix $Q$ remains available for export to
annealing hardware.

\subsubsection{Seeding and restarts} Restarts begin from degree-cap-aware
random spanning trees. Greedy edge-by-edge growth is not used for seeding,
because with connectivity constraints every proper prefix of a feasible tree
can be infeasible, so a greedy builder can stall before it ever reaches a
legal state. The first restart may instead start from a \emph{warm} edge set,
the previous cycle's decision intersected with the live graph.

\subsubsection{Polish} After the temperature schedule ends, a deterministic
polish alternates single-toggle and pairwise-swap passes until no single move
improves the energy, a local fixed point.

Because every candidate is feasible by construction, the penalty walls never
appear in the search; only the soft landscape, with energy differences of
order one, has to be traversed. This is the sense in which projection
\emph{removes} the barrier rather than tuning around it.

The benchmark schedule cools from temperature 1.0 to 0.01 at rate 0.998 with
four steps per temperature and five restarts; inside the closed loop a faster
schedule (rate 0.995, two steps, two restarts) is used, the warm start counting
as one restart. Pseudo-code is given in Appendix~1.

\section{How Good Is the Projected Annealer?}

The projected annealer is deliberately kept \emph{out} of the sampler
comparison above, because its move set is richer than single bit flips and
the comparison would not be like for like. Its own performance is reported
separately, as an engineering result.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
\item On the 25 exactly verifiable instances it finds the exact optimum in
\textbf{100\%} of cases, using about $2.4\times10^{4}$ energy evaluations and
2.1~s per solve.
\item On 20 larger instances with up to 40 optional edges, checked against
exact MILP optima, it is exact in \textbf{95\%} of cases, with a mean
normalised gap of $7\times10^{-5}$ and a worst case of $1.4\times10^{-3}$.
\item On 20 instances with up to 817 binary variables, where no exact optimum
was computed, its gap against the best of four annealer seeds is 6.9\% on
average (90th percentile 17\%), which measures consistency across seeds
rather than distance from the optimum.
\end{enumerate}

Throughout, the normalised gap is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{gap}=\frac{H_{\mathrm{SA}}-H^{*}}{\max\bigl(1,|H^{*}|\bigr)},
\label{eq:sol:gap}
\end{equation}
where the $\max(1,\cdot)$ prevents a near-zero optimum from inflating the
ratio.

\section{Sensitivity to the Encoding's Free Parameters}

The utility weights and penalties are chosen by hand, so a fair question is
whether the solver works only for one lucky setting. On a fixed instance set
the projected annealer is exact on 87\% of instances, and it stays at exactly
87\% when the trust weight is scaled by 1.5, when the utility weights are made
uniform, when all penalties are divided or multiplied by five, when the
sparsity penalty is raised to 0.3 and when the stability penalty is raised to
0.6. The solver is not fragile to the encoding's free parameters.

\section{The Relaxation Ladder}
\label{sec:sol:ladder}

In a live system the constraints can be jointly infeasible: a budget below
the cheapest connected subgraph, or a degree cap of two on a radio graph that
has no chain through every required rover. The production pruner therefore
tries a ladder of progressively relaxed problems and records which rung it
used in every cycle (Table~\ref{tab:sol:ladder}).

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{The relaxation ladder of the production pruner, tried top to
bottom.}
\label{tab:sol:ladder}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l X@{}}
\toprule
Rung & Constraints enforced \\
\midrule
full & degree caps, budget and connectivity of the reachable required rovers \\
no-budget & degree caps and connectivity; budget dropped \\
budget-only & degree caps and budget; connectivity dropped \\
unconstrained & degree caps only \\
keep-all fallback & nothing could be satisfied; every candidate link is kept \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

Two details make the ladder honest. First, the required set is the fixed
mission roster restricted to the rovers currently reachable from the root;
unreachable mission rovers are counted, not silently redefined away. Second,
the rung used is logged every cycle, so the coverage of each guarantee is a
measured number rather than an assertion. That bookkeeping later exposed the
silent budget relaxation of Section~\ref{sec:res:audit}.

\begin{remark}[A restart floor]
The warm start counts as one of the solver's restarts. With a single restart
and an infeasible warm start, such as the very first cycle when every
candidate link is still marked as kept, no random spanning tree is ever
tried, so a perfectly solvable instance falls through every rung of the
ladder to the keep-all fallback. The defect was found while calibrating the
3D engine of Chapter~\ref{ch:engine}, whose minimum number of restarts is
therefore two.
\end{remark}

\section{Implications}

The central finding is that a correct encoding is necessary but not
sufficient. A generic bit-level annealer can fail to recover the optimum of a
correct QUBO because of the very penalty walls the encoding needs, and that
failure is invisible unless solver output is checked against exact ground
truth. Moving the search into the feasible space removes the barrier for the
classical solver. Whether quantum annealing hardware, whose transverse-field
dynamics differ from thermal sampling, behaves better on this landscape is an
open question that the exported Q matrices are ready to answer.
